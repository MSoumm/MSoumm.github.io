# Introduction 
State-of-the-art text-to-image (T2I) generative models <d-cite key="rombach2021highresolution,podell2023sdxl,ramesh2022hierarchical,saharia2022photorealistic,esser2024scalingrectifiedflowtransformers"></d-cite> can produce visually stunning and highly realistic images. However, despite their power, they often struggle with a fundamental task: faithfully aligning the generated image with the user's prompt.

These alignment failures have been widely reported <d-cite key="ramesh2022hierarchical,saharia2022photorealistic,chefer2023attendandexcite,feng2023trainingfreestructureddiffusionguidance"></d-cite> and typically manifest in two common ways:

* **Catastrophic Neglect**: Key objects or elements mentioned in the prompt are completely missing from the final image.

* **Subject Mixing**: Attributes and features of distinct objects are incorrectly blended together.

This can lead to frustrating and incorrect results, even with powerful models like Stable Diffusion 3, significantly limiting their utility.

The central challenge is to correct these errors without the massive computational cost of retraining the entire model. Our work, SAGA, introduces a training-free method that guides the generation process at inference time to ensure the final image accurately reflects every component of the prompt.

# A Quick Recap on Diffusion
Generative frameworks like Diffusion Models <d-cite key="ho2020denoising"></d-cite> and Flow Matching <d-cite key="lipman2023flow"></d-cite> share a core concept: they learn to transform a simple noise distribution into the complex distribution of real images.

The process begins by taking an image, which we consider the clean $\mathbf{z}_0 \sim p_0(\mathbf{z}_0)$ in the model's latent space, and gradually adding Gaussian noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ over a series of timesteps $t \in [0, T]$. This *forward process* is defined by a simple equation:

$$
\mathbf{z}_t = a_t \mathbf{z}_0 + b_t \boldsymbol{\epsilon}
$$

Here, $\mathbf{z}_t$ is the noisy image at step $t$. The coefficients $a_t$ and $b_t$ control a trade-off: as $t$ increases, $a_t$ decreases, fading out the original signal, while $b_t$ increases, fading in the noise. At the final step $T$, the latent becomes indistinguishable from pure noise. In particular, we may assume that:

$$
\underset{t\rightarrow T}{\lim} a_t = 0 \text{ and }\underset{t\rightarrow T}{\lim} b_t = 1
$$

The model is then trained to reverse this process. Given a noisy latent $\mathbf{z}_t$ and a text condition $\mathbf{y}$, the model learns to predict and remove the noise, step-by-step, to recover the original signal $\mathbf{z}_0$, which can be done using many different procedures, including DDIM CITE or DDPM CITE in discrete step formulation, or SDE solvers like DPM++ 2M CITE for more recent models trained with a flow-matching objective.

An interesting capability of these models is that at any intermediate step $t$, we can ask for an estimate of the final clean image, which we denote as $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_0(\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{y}, t)$. Early in the denoising process (i.e. for large $t$), this estimate is a coarse, blurry approximation of the final scene, capturing only the low-frequency structural information. This initial guess is fundamental to understanding how SAGA operates.

# Previous GSN Approaches

To address alignment failures, several training-free methods have been developed to guide the generation process. These can be broadly categorized into two main strategies: course correction during denoising, and optimizing the initial noise.

## Strategy 1: Nudging the Denoising Path (GSN)

The first strategy, known as Generative Semantic Nursing (GSN) <d-cite key="rassin2023linguistic, Agarwal_2023_ICCV, chefer2023attendandexcite, li2023divide, guo2024initno"></d-cite>, can be thought of as "course correction." At various steps during the denoising process, it checks the model's internal attention maps to see if it is correctly focusing on all the concepts in the prompt. If an object is being neglected, GSN applies a small gradient-based "nudge" to the latent image $\mathbf{z}_t$ to steer it back on track.

$$
\mathbf{z}_t \leftarrow \mathbf{z}_t - \alpha_t \cdot \nabla_{\mathbf{z}_t} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{M})
$$

While effective, this constant intervention has a significant drawback. The repeated nudges can push the latent representation into an "unnatural" state that the model wasn't trained to handle, leading to out-of-distribution samples and visual artifacts <d-cite key="guo2024initno"></d-cite>.

## Strategy 2: Optimizing the Starting Point (InitNO)

The second strategy, pioneered by InitNO <d-cite key="guo2024initno"></d-cite>, focuses on finding a better *starting point*. Instead of correcting the path midway, it optimizes the initial noise vector $\mathbf{z}_T$ *before* the denoising process begins. The goal is to find a starting point in the initial noise distribution that is already predisposed to generate a prompt-aligned image.

This avoids the out-of-distribution problem of GSN. However, its intervention occurs at $t=T$, when the latent is pure noise and contains no structural "signal" at all. This makes optimization extremely challenging, as the model is essentially guessing in the dark about which initial noise configuration will lead to a successful outcome.

<figure>
<img src="data/figs/method/ICCV_InitNO.pdf" alt="Diagram showing InitNO optimizing the initial noise z_T.">
<figcaption><b>Figure 4</b>: InitNO optimizes the initial noise $\mathbf{z}_T$ to find a more favorable starting point for the generation, sampling from a specific region of the prior Gaussian distribution.
</figcaption>
</figure>

Both strategies offer partial solutions, but each has fundamental limits. One intervenes repeatedly, risking artifacts; the other intervenes too early, lacking a strong signal to guide it. This highlights the need for a method that can intervene at the optimal moment, with a more principled approach.
